# TD-Gammon: a self-taught backgammon net reaches near-world-class play

**Field:** ♟️ Games & Strategy  ·  **When:** 1992–1995 · Communications of the ACM (March 1995)

> A neural network that learned backgammon by playing itself a million and a half times, with no human games, came within a single point of a world-class player over 40 games and changed how experts open the game.

## What happened
**Gerald Tesauro** at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center trained a neural network to evaluate backgammon positions using **temporal-difference learning, TD(λ)**, on games the network generated by playing against itself. The network received essentially a raw board encoding (198 inputs in the later versions, with a small set of hand-designed features added in 1.0 and later), had 80 hidden units, and output estimates of the probability of each game outcome. No expert games and no human-labelled positions were used; the only training signal was who eventually won.

Tesauro reports the results against human experts in the 1995 *Communications of the ACM* article:

| Version | Training games | Opponents | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 (1991) | 300,000 | Robertie, Magriel, Davis (51 games) | −13 points |
| 2.0 (1992) | 800,000 | Goulding, Woolsey and others (38 games) | −7 points |
| 2.1 (1993) | 1,500,000 | Bill Robertie (40 games) | **−1 point** |

Robertie, a two-time world champion, judged version 2.1 to be playing at "strong master level." A 1998 successor, version 3.1 (160 hidden units, more than 6 million self-play games), lost a 100-game exhibition to **Malcolm Davis by 8 points**, still not quite the best human, but close.

The more lasting effect was on human play. TD-Gammon preferred to **split its back checkers** on several common opening rolls where three decades of expert consensus had favoured "slotting" a checker on the 5-point. Rollout analysis backed the network, and the split plays became standard in tournament backgammon. Kit Woolsey, one of its opponents, wrote that its edge was in "judgment, not calculation."

## Why it matters
TD-Gammon is the earliest system in this collection that *learned* its game, and the first clear demonstration that reinforcement learning from self-play, with a neural network as the value function, could reach expert level in a real game. Every ingredient that made [AlphaGo](../alphago-move-37/) and [AlphaZero](../alphazero-self-play/) possible two decades later is present here in miniature: a learned evaluation, self-play as the data source, and a small amount of lookahead search on top. It also showed, for the first time, a learned system teaching humans something about a game they had studied for centuries.
