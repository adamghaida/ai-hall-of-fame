# Deep Blue beats Kasparov

**Field:** ♟️ Games & Strategy  ·  **When:** May 1997

> The first computer to beat a reigning world chess champion in a match, by brute-force search rather than learning. Deep Blue was not a machine-learning system; it is here as a landmark of computer play, for the record.

## What happened
In **February 1996** in Philadelphia, IBM's **Deep Blue** won the first game of a six-game match against world champion **Garry Kasparov**, the first time a machine had beaten a reigning champion in a game under standard tournament time controls. Kasparov recovered and won that match **4–2** (three wins, two draws, one loss).

IBM rebuilt the machine and the two met again in New York from **May 3 to 11, 1997**. Kasparov won game 1, Deep Blue won game 2, games 3 to 5 were drawn, and Deep Blue won the decisive game 6. Final score: **Deep Blue 3½–2½**.

The 1997 machine was an IBM RS/6000 SP with **480 custom chess chips**, evaluating about **200 million positions per second**. Its evaluation function was written and tuned by hand, with grandmaster Joel Benjamin helping refine its openings. There was no learning from data: the strength came from search depth, purpose-built hardware, and expert-written rules.

## Why it matters
Chess had been the benchmark for machine intelligence since the 1950s, and Kasparov was arguably the strongest player who had ever lived. His defeat settled, in public, that a machine could out-play the best human at the game that had defined "thinking" for computer science. Kasparov's own reaction, per IBM's account, was that the computer was "far stronger than anybody expected."

The result also taught a lesson that the later, learned systems in this collection would confirm from the other direction: raw search on specialized hardware was enough for chess. What Deep Blue could not do was generalize. It played one game, and nothing it "knew" transferred. That gap is the reason [AlphaZero](../alphazero-self-play/) two decades later, learning chess from the rules alone, was a bigger scientific step than a bigger Deep Blue would have been.
