# 📚 Context: Watson wins Jeopardy!

**The challenge.** IBM had beaten a chess champion with Deep Blue in 1997 and wanted a follow-up "grand challenge." *Jeopardy!* was chosen because its clues are deliberately oblique natural language spanning every subject, and because contestants are penalized for wrong answers, so a player must know how sure it is. The project was led by David Ferrucci at IBM Research, and the system was named for IBM's first CEO, Thomas J. Watson Sr.

**How it worked.** DeepQA was a large engineered pipeline rather than a single model. A clue was parsed to work out what kind of thing was being asked for; hundreds of candidate answers were generated by searching the offline corpus; each candidate was scored by many independent evidence-gathering and scoring components; and the scores were merged into a ranked list with a confidence estimate. Watson buzzed only if the top confidence exceeded a threshold that also depended on the game state. Ken Jennings's later remark, quoted by IBM, was that quiz-show contestant might be the first job Watson made redundant.

**The match.** Two games, aired over three nights, February 14 to 16, 2011. A practice round on January 13 went to Watson $4,400, Jennings $3,400, Rutter $1,200 with no wrong answers from anyone. In the real match Watson finished with $77,147 to Jennings's $24,000 and Rutter's $21,600.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
It was the first time a computer beat top humans at an open-domain natural-language task in public, and it did so with the confidence calibration that the game demands. For a general audience it was the first "AI beats humans" moment since Deep Blue, and it reframed AI from board games toward language. For the field, it demonstrated that combining many weak evidence sources with learned weighting could outperform any single method, an idea that carried into search and question-answering products for the following decade.

## Honest caveats
- **Not a learned model in the modern sense.** Watson is included as a landmark of computer play. DeepQA was hand-engineered, component by component, over years; statistical scoring was one ingredient, not the architecture. It is not comparable to the neural systems that follow it in this collection, and IBM's own history page describes it as programmed rather than trained.
- **The buzzer.** Watson received each clue as electronic text at the moment it appeared and pressed the physical buzzer with a solenoid, about 5 to 10 milliseconds after deciding to answer; a typical human reaction to the enabling light is around 190 milliseconds. Top players can beat that by anticipating the light, and both humans did win buzzes. But Rutter's summary, "It was its buzzer that killed us," is widely shared, and on a written test of knowledge the match might well have gone the other way. Producers had already required the physical button after IBM originally proposed a direct electronic signal.
- **Exhibition, not a regular game.** The contest was staged at IBM's own lab under rules agreed with the show. It was a public test of the system, but it was a one-off of two games, and Watson has not played since.
- **Errors of understanding.** The Toronto answer, a US-cities question answered with a Canadian city, is a reminder that the system matched evidence rather than understanding the question; the Wikipedia summary attributes it to parsing difficulties and weak comparative reasoning, including under-weighting the category.
- **Commercial afterlife.** IBM's later "Watson" products shared the brand more than the 2011 system, and their record is a separate story from the match.
