# Watson wins Jeopardy!

**Field:** ♟️ Games & Strategy  ·  **When:** February 2011

> A room-sized IBM system beat the two best Jeopardy! players in history on live television, $77,147 to $24,000 and $21,600, while famously answering "Toronto" to a question about US cities. Watson was an engineered question-answering pipeline, not a learned model in the sense of the later entries here; it is included as a landmark of computer play, for the record.

## What happened
Over three broadcasts on **February 14, 15 and 16, 2011**, IBM's **Watson** played a two-game exhibition match of *Jeopardy!* against **Ken Jennings** and **Brad Rutter**, the show's two most successful players. The match was recorded on January 14, 2011 at IBM's research lab in Yorktown Heights, New York. Watson finished with **$77,147** against Jennings's **$24,000** and Rutter's **$21,600**, taking the **$1 million** first prize, which IBM donated in full to charity.

Watson ran on a cluster of **90 IBM Power 750 servers** (2,880 processor cores, 16 terabytes of RAM) in ten racks, and answered in about three seconds. It had no internet connection during play; its knowledge came from roughly **200 million pages** of text (about 4 terabytes), including Wikipedia, loaded in advance. The software, **DeepQA**, was built by a team of more than two dozen researchers led by **David Ferrucci**. For each clue it generated many candidate answers, gathered evidence for each, scored them, and buzzed only when its confidence cleared a threshold.

Its most-quoted moment was a miss. In the first game's Final Jeopardy, category "U.S. Cities," the correct response was Chicago; Watson wrote "What is Toronto?????", but had wagered only $947 because its confidence was low.

## Why it matters
Jeopardy! clues are written for humans: puns, indirection, categories that change what a clue means. Answering them in seconds, from text alone, with calibrated confidence about when to risk buzzing, was far beyond any question-answering system of the time. Watson showed that open-domain natural-language question answering could reach expert human level on a public, adversarial test, five years before neural language models made that routine, and it put "AI beats humans at language" on the front page for the first time.
