# 📚 Context: Astra's ten results and their Lean certificates

**What Astra is.** Astra is the name OpenAI gave to its next major model family. An unreleased internal version produced the ten results; it is not a public product, there is no API, no testing interface, and no announced release date. Coverage describes it as a multi-agent system aimed at long-horizon tasks. The repository's own `formalization.yaml` records the method as `agent`, the model as `Astra (OpenAI)`, the framework as `Codex`, and the wall time as `1 week`.

**What was published.** Three artifacts, all on August 1, 2026: a [249-page manuscript](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/ten-proofs-oai.pdf) written as ten self-contained chapters with full literature reviews; a [reasoning-walkthroughs PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/reasoning-walkthroughs.pdf) in which the model reconstructs how each proof came together from its own (unpublished) reasoning traces; and the [`openai/ten-proofs`](https://github.com/openai/ten-proofs) repository of Lean 4 formalizations, Apache-2.0, pinned to Lean 4.32.0 and mathlib. Simon Willison called this "a decent level of transparency" while noting he would still like to see the actual prompts.

**Why the Lean part is the interesting part.** The manuscript itself never names Astra and contains no methodology section; it reads as ten ordinary mathematics papers. The verification story lives entirely in the repository. Two structural details are worth knowing:

- `formalization.yaml` names twelve top-level declarations (the ten results, with codes and extremal graphs each contributing two), each with `sorry_count: 0` and an axiom list containing only Lean's three standard axioms: `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`. Independently grepping the ten top-level `.lean` files finds zero occurrences of `sorry`.
- `ComparatorChallenges/` contains standalone Lean files that *restate* each headline theorem from scratch, with the proof left as `sorry`. That is intentional: those files are the challenge statements. The [Comparator](https://github.com/leanprover/comparator) tool checks that the shipped proofs really close exactly those statements, which is how a third party audits the release without trusting OpenAI's own build.

**The division of labour.** OpenAI's framing, as reported, is that the mathematical arguments are the model's, that human researchers turned them into the written manuscripts, and that the model produced the Lean formalizations. OpenAI explicitly declined to claim human authorship, on the grounds that doing so would misrepresent both the system's contribution and the nature of human intellectual work.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
Ten results, each in a different corner of mathematics or theoretical computer science, each open for at least a decade and several for much longer, released simultaneously with a machine-checkable proof object. The sphere-packing chapter claims the first improvement to the general high-dimensional packing exponent since 1978. The non-sofic group construction settles a question that goes back to Gromov's 1999 introduction of soficity and Weiss's subsequent question, and which had previously only been reachable through unproved stability hypotheses. Whatever the eventual verdict on any individual chapter, the *format* of the release, proof plus certificate plus independent-checker configs, is the strongest verification package any AI lab has attached to a mathematics claim so far.

## Honest caveats

- **Formalization fidelity is the whole ballgame.** This is the caveat that matters most, and it is easy to miss. A successful Lean build proves that the Lean statement follows from the axioms. It does **not** prove that the Lean statement is the conjecture mathematicians thought was open. Four separate gaps have to be closed by human specialists: *statement fidelity* (does the formal theorem say what the abstract says?), *definition fidelity* (do the encoded objects match standard usage in the field?), *reduction fidelity* (where the formal endpoint is narrower than the headline, is the informal reduction that bridges them actually complete?), and *novelty* (is the result new, and is its historical framing accurate?). None of these is machine-checkable. "Zero `sorry`s" is strong evidence against hallucinated proof steps and no evidence at all about any of the four. There is also a granularity point worth stating plainly: "a Lean certificate for every result" is true at the level of OpenAI's ten headline results, not at the level of every headline sub-claim. Result 5, for example, states two bounds — Ω(n² log log n) gates for division-free circuits and Ω(n⁴ / log n) leaves for formulas — but `formalization.yaml` lists exactly one Permanent declaration, `permanent_rational_formula_logarithmic_lower_bound` ("Permanent lower bounds for formulas with division"); the circuit-gate bound has no listed main declaration of its own.
- **Not peer reviewed, and self-reviewed.** As of publication no independent specialist review or peer-reviewed publication of the bundle existed. The repository's own metadata sets `review: status: "agent-reviewed"`, meaning the review pass was itself done by a model.
- **The Erdős database has not caught up.** Results 9 and 10 are described here as resolving Erdős problems 183, 146 and 180, following the manuscript's own framing. As of this writing that is not yet reflected in the official [erdosproblems.com](https://www.erdosproblems.com/) database, where all three are still listed as **OPEN** — problem [183](https://www.erdosproblems.com/183) last edited 10 April 2026, [146](https://www.erdosproblems.com/146) and [180](https://www.erdosproblems.com/180) last edited 18 January 2026, none of them touched since the August 1 release. The `teorth/erdosproblems` wiki that tracks AI contributions now states it is no longer updated, with data only as of 30 June 2026, so the database itself is the live check.
- **Undisclosed failures and prompts.** OpenAI did not say how many problems were attempted without a solution. The reasoning walkthroughs show successful branches only. The prompts, the search system, the checkpoints, and the execution environment are all unpublished, and Astra is unavailable, so nobody outside OpenAI can reproduce the *search* even though anybody can check the *proofs*.
- **The cost figure is narrow, and sources disagree on its scope.** Most coverage reports roughly $2,000 in tokens at GPT-5.6 Sol API rates as the total across all ten problems; Simon Willison's writeup reads OpenAI's wording as under $2,000 *per problem*. Either way, the figure is marginal inference pricing only. It excludes training, infrastructure, the human researchers' time, and every failed research direction.
- **The community is already on edge about exactly this.** The [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/), published June 2, 2026 by sixteen mathematicians and endorsed by the International Mathematical Union, asks that AI use be disclosed, that AI-assisted papers be peer reviewed, and that academia be resourced to keep pace with corporate labs. Its signatory page listed 3,365 signatories at the time of writing; named endorsers include Kevin Buzzard, Leslie Ann Goldberg, Peter Scholze, and Ulrike Tillmann. A press-release-first announcement of ten major theorems is precisely the pattern the declaration was written about.
- **OpenAI has been wrong here before.** In October 2025, then-VP Kevin Weil posted that GPT-5 had solved ten open Erdős problems. Thomas Bloom, who maintains erdosproblems.com, pointed out that the model had surfaced existing published solutions rather than proving anything new, and called the announcement a dramatic misrepresentation; the post was deleted and Demis Hassabis called the episode embarrassing. That history is the reason Bloom's reaction to *this* release matters: he called the ten new results "big news" and rated them above the unit-distance counterexample OpenAI disclosed in May 2026, which he had helped verify. His posted wording is worth keeping precise: he ranked the release above the unit-distance *counterexample*, while adding that it was maybe not bigger than a *proof* of unit distance would have been — "in terms of constructions, this is big."
- **Ten results, ten different specialist communities.** Sphere packing, operator algebras, group theory, circuit complexity, quantum information, lattice cryptography, and extremal graph theory do not share referees. Verification will arrive at different speeds for different chapters, and it is entirely possible that some hold up completely while others turn out to be weaker or better-known than the framing suggests.
