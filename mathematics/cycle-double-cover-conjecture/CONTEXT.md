# 📚 Context: the Cycle Double Cover Conjecture and OpenAI's claimed proof

**The conjecture.** A *cycle* here is a connected 2-regular subgraph, so two parallel edges count as a cycle of length two. A *bridge* is an edge whose removal disconnects the graph, and a bridge can never lie on a cycle, so being bridgeless is obviously necessary. The **cycle double cover conjecture** says that condition is also sufficient: every finite bridgeless graph has a multiset of cycles in which every edge appears in exactly two of them. Per Sang-il Oum's exposition, it was stated independently by Paul Seymour in 1979 for all bridgeless graphs and by George Szekeres in 1973 for bridgeless cubic graphs, with an equivalent version due to Itai and Rodeh; OpenAI's PDF also credits Tutte. It is one of the standard named open problems of graph theory, and it is implied by the stronger Strong Embedding Conjecture (reduce to 2-connected graphs and take all face boundaries), which remains open.

Before July 2026 it was known for planar graphs (take facial boundaries), for 3-edge-colourable cubic graphs (take the three unions of pairs of colour classes), and for bridgeless graphs with no Petersen subdivision (Alspach, Goddyn and Zhang, 1994). A minimum counterexample had to be a snark.

**The announcement.** On July 10, 2026, OpenAI researcher Ethan Knight posted on X that GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra, released to general availability the day before, had produced a proof "using 64 subagents in just under one hour," and linked two PDFs on OpenAI's CDN: the [proof](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/04d1d1e4-bc75-476a-97cf-49055cd98d31/cdc_proof.pdf) and the [prompt](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/04d1d1e4-bc75-476a-97cf-49055cd98d31/cdc_prompt.pdf). There was no dedicated openai.com blog post. The proof PDF's author line is "OPENAI"; its statement of AI use credits the proof entirely to GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra and the writeup to Codex running GPT-5.6 Sol. The [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48863490) posted the same day reached 538 points and hundreds of comments.

**The prompt.** OpenAI released the full prompt, which is unusual and worth reading. It fixes definitions tightly, states plainly that "partial progress does not count," and pre-emptively rejects the standard near-miss outcomes: special graph classes, covers with some edges covered other than twice, bounded-length or prescribed-cycle variants, reductions to another unproved conjecture, computational verification up to a fixed size, and candidate counterexamples without a nonexistence certificate. It then gives search-management instructions: use up to 64 concurrent agents dynamically rather than by fixed assignment, start from a genuinely diverse portfolio of formulations, withhold the currently favored approach from most agents to preserve independence, maintain a registry of approach families and redirect agents away from crowded ones, mark routes blocked when they stall at a theorem-strength missing lemma, and run adversarial agents that specifically check for exact-two multiplicity, closed trails masquerading as cycles, parallel-edge 2-cycles, disconnected graphs, cut vertices, bridges introduced by reductions, and circular use of an equivalent CDC statement. Web search is permitted for standard background only, not to look up a solution to this conjecture. It ends with "spend at least 8 hours on this before even thinking of returning or giving up" and forbids returning a partial result. That the run finished in under an hour despite the eight-hour instruction was itself a topic of discussion.

**The mathematics.** The published argument is three pages. It reduces to loopless cubic multigraphs, invokes the Jaeger-Kilpatrick result that every bridgeless graph has a nowhere-zero flow valued in Γ = F₂³ (equivalently a nowhere-zero 8-flow, by Tutte's group-flow theorem), and then does something clean: it converts that flow into an assignment of a **two-element subset** P_e ⊆ Γ to each edge, arranged so that at every vertex each group element of Γ appears either zero or two times. Given that, the sets M_s = {e : s ∈ P_e} are disjoint unions of cycles and their components form a cycle double cover. The condition is a relaxation of a proper 3-edge-colouring, which is what makes it achievable on snarks. The only real work is showing the local choices can be made to agree across every edge, which becomes a linear system over F₂ and is settled by a duality and parity argument.

**Independent engagement.** Within a week, two senior structural graph theorists posted expository write-ups. **Jim Geelen** (Waterloo) posted [arXiv:2607.15399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15399) on July 16, describing it as his attempt to clarify the OpenAI proof to himself and offering no new insights, just an easier read. **Sang-il Oum** (IBS Discrete Mathematics Group) posted [arXiv:2607.16356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16356) on July 17, revised July 23, presenting a mostly self-contained version aimed at advanced undergraduates; he modified two parts, making the choice of two values at each edge symmetric and replacing the dual-vector-space argument with the elementary fact that a matrix's column space is the orthogonal complement of its left null space. Neither reports an error. Oum also gave a seminar talk on it. Separately, Radek Hušek and Robert Šámal posted [arXiv:2607.24724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24724) on July 27, using the same system of linear equations from OpenAI's proof to derive exponential lower bounds on the number of circuit double covers, which is the first sign of the argument being used as a tool rather than only examined.

**The Lean formalization.** OpenAI published [openai/cdc-lean](https://github.com/openai/cdc-lean), whose endpoint theorem `CDCLean.cycleDoubleCover_of_bridgeless` in `CDCLean/Main.lean` kernel-checks an unconditional cycle double cover theorem for finite loopless bridgeless multigraphs. Lean is pinned to v4.31.0 with a pinned Mathlib revision; the repo's own audit should surface only `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound`, and the source scan should find no `sorry`, `admit`, `native_decide`, or project-specific axioms. The development also proves its two main inputs in-repo rather than assuming them, the Jaeger-Kilpatrick eight-flow theorem in `CDCLean/JaegerKilpatrick.lean` and Nash-Williams-Tutte tree packing in `CDCLean/NashWilliams.lean`, and it declares no `axiom`, `opaque`, or `unsafe` of its own. The repository was created June 24, 2026, its initial commit already titled "Formalize Seymour 6-flow implication to CDC," and the commits establishing the unconditional theorem land on July 9, the day before the public announcement.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
A named, decades-old conjecture from the standard lists of open problems in graph theory, with a complete proof attributed to the model itself rather than to a human using it as an assistant, produced in under an hour, and shipped with the exact prompt, a machine-checked Lean development, and enough transparency for outsiders to audit all of it. Whether or not the community eventually stamps it as settled, the *artifact* is the most checkable AI proof claim on a famous open problem so far, and the released prompt is a documented recipe for multi-agent mathematical search.

## Honest caveats
- **This is a claim, not an accepted theorem.** It has not been peer reviewed or published in a journal, and no authoritative body has declared the cycle double cover conjecture settled. Read every summary that says "AI solved a 50-year-old problem" with that in mind. This entry records a claim and its verification status, not a confirmed resolution.
- **The cycle double cover conjecture attracts failed proofs.** Multiple claimed proofs have been posted over the years and later found to have gaps or were withdrawn. That history is a good reason for the field to move slowly here, independent of who or what produced the argument.
- **The Lean formalization is strong evidence but not a closing argument.** It was produced by OpenAI, not by an independent party, and a sorry-free kernel check only guarantees that the *formal statement as written* follows from the axioms. Someone still has to read the Lean definitions of cycle, bridgeless, and cycle double cover and confirm they mean what the classical conjecture means. Its commits also predate the public announcement, so the public record does not establish that formalization happened during the one-hour run.
- **Missing citations.** Thomas Bloom of the University of Manchester called it "a very nice proof" that is "short, elementary, and could have been discovered in the 1980s," and attributed the model's success partly to persistence where a human would have abandoned an approach. He also criticized the write-up for omitting citations, in particular to a 1983 paper by Bermond, Jackson and Jaeger whose ideas he says underlie the core reduction, and described this as a frequent issue with AI-generated papers. That matters substantively: without provenance it is hard to tell how much is new and how much is retrieved and recombined.
- **The reported numbers are OpenAI's own.** "Under an hour," "64 subagents," and the attribution of the mathematics entirely to the model are unaudited self-reports. There is no public record of how many other conjectures were attempted and failed, how many prompt variants were tried, what the run cost, or what the intermediate agent transcripts contained. Only the final polished proof was released.
- **The expositions are clarifications, not certifications.** Geelen and Oum both wrote up the argument without reporting a gap, which is meaningful and is roughly the informal process by which short proofs get accepted. But neither paper is a formal referee report, and "no gap reported yet" is weaker than "verified correct."
