# 📚 Context: SynTnpBs, AI-designed compact genome editors

**The protein.** **TnpB** is a small, transposon-encoded, RNA-guided DNA nuclease, roughly a third the size of Cas9 and widely regarded as the evolutionary ancestor of the CRISPR-Cas12 family. Size is the point: gene editing is often bottlenecked not by cutting but by delivery, and a hypercompact editor fits into vectors that will not accommodate a full Cas9 or Cas12. The trade-off has been that natural TnpB is a mediocre editor in mammalian cells. Several groups have tried to improve it by mutational scanning and directed evolution; this paper tried computational redesign instead.

**The method.** The team used **ESM Inverse Folding (ESM-IF1)**, a model that runs in the opposite direction from a structure predictor: give it a backbone geometry and it proposes amino acid sequences likely to adopt that geometry. Left alone, an inverse-folding model happily rewrites residues that a nuclease cannot afford to lose. So the authors layered **evolution-informed residue constraints** on top, holding fixed the positions that natural sequence conservation flags as important for contacting the guide RNA and the target DNA, and letting the model rewrite the rest. The designs were assembled combinatorially from redesigned protein parts and screened in bulk.

**The people.** The paper's first two authors, **Petr Skopintsev** and **Isabel Esain-Garcia**, with **Evan DeTurk** and a large team at the **Innovative Genomics Institute** and **UC Berkeley**; senior author **Jennifer Doudna**, who shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for CRISPR-Cas9. Trade coverage framed the paper as the Doudna lab entering the AI protein design arena ([FierceBiotech](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/research/nobel-laureate-jennifer-doudna-enters-ai-protein-design-arena)) — a field it had largely watched from the outside while the Baker lab and Meta's ESM team defined it.

**The lineage.** This entry sits downstream of two others in this collection. [RFdiffusion](../rfdiffusion-protein-design/) showed that generative models can invent protein folds that do not exist in nature. [ESM3](../esm3-esmgfp/) showed a language model producing a functional fluorescent protein far from anything natural. SynTnpBs are a narrower but harder-nosed result: not a new fold, but a machine-rewritten version of a real molecular machine, benchmarked head-to-head against the natural enzyme on the task the natural enzyme evolved to do, and then solved structurally.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
Most generative protein design results are measured against themselves: does the design fold, does it bind, does it express. This one was measured against evolution's version of the same enzyme, in three different kinds of living cell, and won on efficiency at some targets while the AI-written portions of the protein diverged by roughly a fifth to a quarter from anything natural. The cryo-EM structures then showed *why* it won: new contacts stabilizing the RNA-DNA interface that were not in the original. Design, validation, and mechanism in one paper is rare.

## Honest caveats
- **No therapeutic use, and none close.** These are research-stage enzymes. Nothing here has been tested for safety, delivery in a whole organism, immunogenicity, or durability of editing. Cultured HEK293T cells and *Arabidopsis* protoplasts are the easiest possible proving grounds.
- **Constrained redesign, not de novo invention.** The fold, the guide RNA, the catalytic mechanism, and the nucleic-acid-contacting residues all came from a natural protein. The model rewrote the scaffold around a fixed functional core. Calling the output "AI-designed" is defensible, but it is a weaker claim than designing an enzyme from nothing, and the paper's own framing ("structure and evolution-guided") reflects that.
- **The headline numbers are from secondary coverage.** The Science paper is paywalled. The abstract states only that screening yielded editors that "retained or exceeded wild-type activity in bacterial, plant, and human cells" and that cryo-EM of the most divergent variant revealed stabilizing contacts in the RNA-DNA interfaces across conformations. The specific figures repeated in this entry (1,980 designed protein-part combinations, 466 active, roughly 8% of those active designs beating wild type, 46% and 50% editing versus 28%, close to fourfold at some targets, 83% and 72% lobe identities) come from the **Innovative Genomics Institute's own writeup** of the work, with **Phys.org** reporting the same numbers; the 83% and 72% lobe identities appear independently in **[GEN](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/genome-editing/ai-designed-synthetic-crispr-like-nucleases-show-activity-in-cells/)**. The HEK293T and *Arabidopsis* details come from **CRISPR Medicine News**. Anyone citing them should check the paper.
- **The hit rate cuts both ways.** 466 of 1,980 combinations showing detectable activity is a good yield for redesign at this level of divergence, but it also means most designs failed. This remains a screening pipeline with a model in front of it, not a system that reliably designs a working enzyme on the first try.
- **"First" is narrow.** The claim in the AAAS release is specifically the first experimentally determined structures of **AI-designed RNA-guided nucleases**. Experimental structures of AI-designed proteins in general are not new.
- **Specificity claims need independent replication.** [CRISPR Medicine News](https://crisprmedicinenews.com/news/ai-redesigns-minimal-crispr-nucleases/) reports only that the best variants maintained the expected **target recognition sequence** (the TAM preference); the broader **specificity** claim rests on the [Innovative Genomics Institute's writeup](https://innovativegenomics.org/news/ai-designed-functional-genome-editors/). Comprehensive off-target profiling of a new editor is normally its own study, and independent labs have not yet reproduced these results.
- **Bacterial screening is a biased filter.** Selecting first in *E. coli* enriches for designs that express and fold well in bacteria, which is not the same population as designs that would work best in human cells.
