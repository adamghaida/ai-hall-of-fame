# 📚 Context: Evo-generated bacteriophage genomes

**The problem.** Generative models in biology had, by 2025, become good at single molecules. [RFdiffusion](../rfdiffusion-protein-design/) invents protein folds; [AlphaFold](../../chemistry/alphafold-protein-structure/) predicts their structures; language models write plausible enzyme sequences. A genome is a different object. It is not one molecule but a program: genes that must be present in the right number, in the right order, with functioning promoters, packaging signals, and — in the case of ΦX174 — overlapping reading frames where one stretch of DNA encodes two different proteins in two different frames. Get any of it wrong and nothing happens at all. Whether a sequence model that had only ever been scored on perplexity and on single-element design could produce a working genome was an open question.

**The choice of target.** ΦX174 is the natural place to try. It is a small lytic bacteriophage of *E. coli*, one of the most studied genomes in biology, and small enough to synthesize commercially as a single construct. It is also lytic, which gives an unambiguous readout: transform the synthesized DNA into *E. coli*, and either the culture clears or it does not.

**The method.** The team fine-tuned **Evo 1** and **Evo 2** — genome-scale DNA language models from the Arc Institute — on roughly 15,000 Microviridae genomes, the family ΦX174 belongs to. They then sampled whole genomes from the fine-tuned models, filtered them computationally for realistic genetic architecture and for the right host tropism, ordered DNA synthesis, and transformed the results into *E. coli* C. Of **302 generated genomes, 285 were successfully synthesized**; **16 produced viable phages**.

**The people.** First author **Samuel H. King** with **Claudia L. Driscoll**, **David B. Li**, **Daniel Guo**, **Aditi T. Merchant**, **Garyk Brixi**, **Max E. Wilkinson**, and corresponding author **Brian L. Hie**, at the **Arc Institute** and **Stanford University**. Hie also leads the Evo model line. The competing-interests statement discloses that Hie is a scientific co-founder of Arpelos Biosciences and Genyro, and that King and Hie are named on a provisional patent application related to the manuscript.

**Publication history.** Posted to bioRxiv on **September 17, 2025** as *"Generative design of novel bacteriophages with genome language models"*, and published in **Science** on **August 6, 2026** as *"Generative design of bacteriophages with genome language models"* (DOI 10.1126/science.aec2657). Note that "novel" was dropped from the title between the two versions.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
This is the first time a generative model has written an entire genome that boots. Not a gene, not a protein, not a promoter, but a complete self-replicating biological program that assembles a capsid, packages its own DNA, and lyses a host. Several of the generated phages then outperformed the natural template they were derived from in direct competition, and one of them turned out — on cryo-EM inspection — to be using a DNA packaging protein from an evolutionarily distant relative, a substitution the model made and the experimenters only discovered afterwards. The cocktail experiment closed the loop on a real use: three *E. coli* strains that had evolved resistance to ΦX174 were cleared by mixtures of its machine-written descendants.

## Honest caveats
- **The success rate is about 5.6%.** 16 viable phages out of 285 synthesized designs. Dr. Simon Jackson (Waikato University) put it as "only around 5% of the designs worked," and Jordi García Ojalvo (Pompeu Fabra University) noted the "efficiency of the process is low: out of thousands generated, only 16 viable phages" ([Science Media Centre](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-generative-design-of-bacteriophages-with-genome-language-models/)). This is a screening pipeline with a good generator in front of it, not a design tool that works first time.
- **Generation within a family, not invention of a new virus.** The generated genomes sit at **93.0%–98.8% nucleotide identity** to training sequences, carrying 67–392 novel mutations. That is meaningful novelty for a 5 kb genome, but it is recombination-and-mutation-scale novelty, not a new viral architecture. The model was fine-tuned on Microviridae and produced Microviridae.
- **ΦX174 is the easy case.** It is among the smallest genomes in biology. Prof. Patrick Cai (University of Manchester) framed the open problem as scaling "these approaches to much larger" genomes with "rigorous experimental validation." Nothing here shows the method works at bacterial or eukaryotic genome scale.
- **Not a therapy.** These are *E. coli* phages tested in culture. There is no animal data, no safety work, no pharmacology. Phage therapy itself remains regulatory-difficult in most jurisdictions.
- **Genuine dual-use tension.** The authors state they "deliberately withheld all viruses with eukaryotic hosts, including those pathogenic to humans, from the models' training data," and recommend that future whole-genome design work "consult both safety and security professionals throughout the project lifecycle." Dr. Simon Clarke (University of Reading) still called out "serious regulatory and safety concerns," warning that future groups may not exercise the same restraint. García Ojalvo's counterpoint is that the low efficiency is itself a barrier, because candidates must be tested individually. Science ran an accompanying commentary by Inglesby and Hanke on exactly this.
- **A reporting discrepancy worth knowing.** The preprint states the cocktail cleared **three** resistant strains (CR1, CR2, CR3, after one, two, and five passages respectively). The AAAS release for the published version says "some combinations of the new phages overcame resistance in **two** strains of *E. coli*." This entry uses the preprint's own numbers; anyone citing the figure should check the published paper.
- **The published version is paywalled.** science.org returned 403 to direct fetching, so the detailed numbers in this entry are quoted from the **bioRxiv preprint full text** and corroborated where possible against press coverage of the Science version. The 285-synthesized and 16-viable figures do appear in coverage of the published paper; the per-strain passage counts and the 93.0–98.8% identity range were verified only in the preprint.
- **Structure accessions were pending.** The preprint's cryo-EM deposition codes were placeholders at the time of posting, so the PDB/EMDB entries are not cited here.
