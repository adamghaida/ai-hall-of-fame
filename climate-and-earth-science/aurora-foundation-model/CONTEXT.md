# 📚 Context: Aurora, a foundation model for the Earth system

**The idea.** By 2024 it was established that a neural network trained on ECMWF's ERA5 reanalysis could match or beat the IFS at medium-range weather ([GraphCast](../graphcast-weather/), Pangu-Weather). Each of those models was a bespoke system for one task. Aurora asked whether the pattern that transformed language and vision, a large model pretrained on diverse data and then fine-tuned, would work for the Earth system, where the datasets differ in variables, resolution, physics and length of record. The paper's answer is that pretraining on a broad mix (ERA5 plus other reanalyses, forecasts and climate simulations) produces a model that fine-tunes to new tasks with far less data and compute than training from scratch, and that the benefit of pretraining is largest exactly where the task-specific data are scarce.

**The people.** The work came from Microsoft Research's AI for Science group. Cristian Bodnar is first author; the author list includes Wessel Bruinsma, Ana Lucic, Megan Stanley, Johannes Brandstetter, Jonathan Weyn, Haiyu Dong, Max Welling, Richard Turner (University of Cambridge) and Paris Perdikaris as last author. The preprint appeared on arXiv in May 2024 (2405.13063) and the peer-reviewed version in *Nature* on 21 May 2025.

**The architecture.** Aurora is a 1.3-billion-parameter model with an encoder, processor and decoder. A Perceiver-style encoder maps whatever set of input variables it is given onto a shared latent representation on a grid of tokens; a 3D Swin transformer advances that representation in time; a decoder reconstructs the variables requested. Because the encoder and decoder learn per-variable embeddings, new variables (ocean waves, atmospheric chemistry species) can be added at fine-tuning time. Pretraining ran for 150,000 steps on 32 A100 GPUs; fine-tuning for a downstream task is much cheaper.

**The four results.** Air quality (against CAMS, ECMWF's operational atmospheric composition system): matches or beats on 74% of targets, competitive within 20% RMSE on 95%. Ocean waves (against HRES-WAM): matches or beats on 86% of wave variables. Tropical cyclone tracks: a single deterministic run beats the official agency forecasts on all cyclones globally in 2022 and 2023, with 6% improvement at one day and 20 to 25% at two to five days in the North Atlantic and East Pacific. High-resolution weather at 0.1°: lower RMSE than IFS HRES on 92% of target combinations, with the largest gains (up to 24%) beyond 12 hours.

**Aurora 1.5.** On 9 July 2026 Microsoft posted a preprint (Weyn et al.) describing a three-stage fine-tuning of Aurora: expanding the surface variable set from 4 to 26, randomising lead-time embeddings to get native hourly output, then injecting Gaussian noise into the adaptive layer-norm modules and training on a CRPS objective to produce an ensemble, a technique the authors credit to ECMWF's AIFS-CRPS. Aurora 1.5 ENS is reported to beat ECMWF's ENS on 88.9% of upper-air and single-level targets over days 1 to 10 with slight over-dispersion, to reduce tropical cyclone track error by 16% relative to the original Aurora, and to reduce mean absolute error on top-5th-percentile heat waves by 58%. Microsoft's blog gives a second cyclone figure, roughly one-third lower track error for the ensemble median against the original Aurora, which is a different comparison from the 16% deterministic figure in the paper's abstract. The model is on GitHub, Hugging Face and Microsoft Foundry.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
Aurora is the first peer-reviewed demonstration that a single pretrained Earth-system model can be fine-tuned to beat the operational systems in four distinct domains, including two (atmospheric chemistry and ocean waves) that earlier AI weather models did not touch. The strength of the result is its breadth rather than any single score.

## Honest caveats
- **Still downstream of physics.** The paper says plainly that "Aurora does still rely on initial conditions from traditional data assimilation systems." Every result starts from an ECMWF analysis. Aurora extends the conventional forecasting pipeline rather than replacing it.
- **Short lead times favour the IFS.** For the 0.1° weather task, "at the shortest lead times, IFS HRES outperforms Aurora for many targets." The 92% figure is over all lead times; the advantage is concentrated beyond 12 hours.
- **"Matches or outperforms" includes ties.** The 74% (air quality) and 86% (waves) headline numbers count targets where Aurora is at least as good as the operational system, and the complementary "within 20% RMSE" figures show that on a minority of targets Aurora is clearly worse. CAMS in particular remains better on roughly a quarter of targets.
- **Deterministic.** The 2025 paper's Aurora produces a single forecast, not an ensemble, so it does not quantify uncertainty the way [GenCast](../gencast-ensemble-weather/) or ECMWF's ENS do. The cyclone-track comparison is a single deterministic run against agency official forecasts, which themselves blend ensembles and human judgement. Ensembles arrive only with Aurora 1.5.
- **Aurora 1.5 is a company preprint.** The 88.9%, 16% and 58% figures are Microsoft's own, from a July 2026 preprint that had not been peer-reviewed at the time of writing. Treat them as claims awaiting independent verification; the peer-reviewed record is the 2025 Nature paper.
- **Two cyclone numbers.** As noted above, Microsoft's blog (about one-third, ensemble median vs original Aurora) and the Aurora 1.5 abstract (16%, vs original Aurora) report different track-error reductions for different comparisons. The entry keeps the abstract's figure as primary.
- **Speed claims are not spelled out here.** The abstract says "orders of magnitude smaller computational expense" than the dedicated systems; specific speed-up multipliers are given in the paper for individual tasks but are not reproduced in this entry because they depend on the hardware baseline chosen.
- **Not operational.** Unlike AIFS or the [NOAA models](../ai-weather-models-operational/), Aurora is a research release. Its results are retrospective evaluations, not a season of live forecasts verified by an agency.
