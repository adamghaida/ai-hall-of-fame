# 📚 Context: AI weather models going operational

**The gap this closes.** [GraphCast](../graphcast-weather/) (2023) and [GenCast](../gencast-ensemble-weather/) (2024) established that machine-learned models could beat the world's best physics-based systems on headline verification scores. Neither was an operational forecast. Operational means something much narrower and much harder: the model runs on a fixed schedule on agency hardware, its output is pushed to public data servers with documented file formats, forecasters build products on top of it, and a written change notice tells thousands of downstream users what is changing and when. That is the transition documented here.

**Who went first.** ECMWF took **AIFS Single v1** operational on **25 February 2025**, after a pre-operational phase, and its implementation notice describes AIFS Single v1 as "the first machine-learning-driven forecast model to be made fully operational." The 51-member **AIFS ENS** followed on **1 July 2025**. ECMWF frames AIFS and the physics-based IFS as complementary rather than competing, with AIFS running at coarser resolution (roughly 28 to 31 km) than the physics ensemble's 9 km.

**What NOAA actually shipped.** NWS Service Change Notice 25-89, issued 9 December 2025 by David Michaud, Director of NCEP Central Operations, announced implementation of AIGFS v1.0, AIGEFS v1.0, and HGEFS v1.0 effective 17 December 2025 at the 1200 UTC cycle, following an evaluation period that began 9 December. The notice states plainly that "the models are based on Google DeepMind's GraphCast model," and NOAA's news release adds that the team fine-tuned GraphCast using NOAA's own Global Data Assimilation System analyses, which improved performance when running from GFS-based initial conditions. Development came out of **Project EAGLE**, a joint effort of the National Weather Service, OAR research labs, the Environmental Modeling Center, and the Earth Prediction Innovation Center. Technical detail sits in NCEP Office Notes 521 ([doi:10.25923/xd3y-wy31](https://doi.org/10.25923/xd3y-wy31)) and 522 ([doi:10.25923/7kpr-5e68](https://doi.org/10.25923/7kpr-5e68)).

**The hybrid is the interesting part.** HGEFS is a 62-member grand ensemble: 31 AI members plus 31 physics members. Because the two systems make structurally different errors, pooling them samples forecast uncertainty better than either does alone, and NOAA reports the combination consistently outperforms both parents across most major verification metrics. This is a direct rebuttal to the "AI replaces physics" framing, and it rhymes with, without being the same thing as, the hybrid design in [NeuralGCM](../neuralgcm/) - HGEFS pools members from two independently built forecast systems, whereas NeuralGCM embeds neural networks inside a physics solver.

**The hurricane season.** The National Hurricane Center used AI weather prediction models in real-time operations for the first time in 2025, including a model developed in partnership with Google DeepMind, NOAA's own AI ensemble, and ECMWF's AI system. The distinction this entry turns on applies here too: none of these were operational NHC models. NWS says the DeepMind model "was used experimentally during the 2025 season" and that the other AI systems were "available for evaluation"; Google DeepMind's Weather Lab, which supplied the DeepMind forecasts, states that "live predictions shown are generated by models still under development and are not official warnings." Forecasters used the AI output as experimental guidance alongside their existing tools when preparing the official forecast. The season featured 13 tropical storms, 5 hurricanes, and three Category 5 hurricanes, the second-highest Category 5 count on record. NHC's verification report singles out the Google DeepMind ensemble mean (GDMI) as slightly outperforming the official forecast for Atlantic track at 12 to 72 hours, and as beating the official forecast and all consensus aids for eastern North Pacific track at 48 to 120 hours. Google DeepMind's own write-up of Hurricane Melissa reports that its WeatherNext model indicated a Category 5 landfall five days ahead with 80% confidence, rising toward certainty three days out.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
Not because an AI model scored well, but because national agencies with legal responsibility for public warnings put AI models into daily operations, published the change notices, and then published an independent verification showing where the AI won and where it did not. That is the full loop: claim, deployment, and audited outcome.

## Honest caveats
- **AI models are not independent of the physics-based system.** They are trained on reanalysis and initialised from operational data assimilation output. Remove the observing network, the assimilation system, and the reanalysis archive, and these models have nothing to run on. This is an extension of conventional numerical weather prediction, not a replacement for it.
- **Track, not intensity.** NOAA states outright that AIGFS v1.0 "shows a degradation in tropical cyclone intensity forecasts" relative to the traditional GFS, and says work continues on HGEFS hurricane intensity. GDMI did well on Atlantic intensity in 2025, but NHC's official forecast still beat every model at 12 and 96 hours and matched GDMI elsewhere.
- **The human forecast is still the benchmark.** NHC's report says official forecasts "generally outperformed most dynamical and consensus models," and its consistency analysis (Figures 10 and 37, drawn from a 2023-25 sample) finds OFCL more consistent cycle to cycle than GFSI, EMXI, UKMI, HCCA and the TVCA/TVCE consensus aids. GDMI is not part of that comparison - it did not exist before the 2025 season. NHC Science Operations Officer Wallace Hogsett's answer on whether AI will replace human forecasters is "a resounding 'no'."
- **Incomplete sample.** GDMI was not available during the early part of the 2025 season, so the homogeneous Atlantic comparison excludes the first two storms and the eastern North Pacific comparison excludes the first five. NHC also notes some AI systems "were not consistently available on a timely basis for routine use by forecasters." Sample sizes shrink sharply with lead time (59 Atlantic forecasts at 120 h).
- **Rare events are the weak spot.** Models learn from what is in the training archive. Genuinely unprecedented events are by definition underrepresented, and a single strong season is not evidence that the models handle the tails.
- **Slight internal inconsistency in NOAA's own release.** The summary bullet says AIGEFS shows "improved performance over the traditional GEFS, extending forecast skill by an additional 18 to 24 hours," while the detailed section for the same model says its "forecast skill is comparable to the operational GEFS." Treat the AIGEFS skill gain as unsettled; the compute saving (9% of GEFS) and the HGEFS hybrid gain are the better-supported claims.
- **One season is one season.** Year-to-year variability in hurricane verification is large, and NHC itself cautions against reading a single model's success or failure as indicative of its overall value.
