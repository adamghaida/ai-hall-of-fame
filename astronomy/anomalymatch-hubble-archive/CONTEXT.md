# 📚 Context: AnomalyMatch and the Hubble Legacy Archive sweep

**The people.** **David O'Ryan** (lead and corresponding author) and **Pablo Gómez**, both at the European Space Agency's European Space Astronomy Centre (ESAC) near Madrid. The underlying method paper, *AnomalyMatch: Discovering Rare Objects of Interest with Semi-supervised and Active Learning*, is by **Pablo Gómez, Laslo E. Ruhberg, Maria Teresa Nardone, and David O'Ryan**, and was accepted for publication in RASTI. The archive-sweep paper is **A&A 704, A227 (2025)**, received 14 May 2025, accepted 13 October 2025, published 16 December 2025. ESA/Hubble and NASA both published press releases on 27 January 2026, which is when most coverage appeared.

**The problem it solves.** Anomaly detection in astronomy is a needle-in-a-haystack problem with almost no labels: you have a handful of known examples of the thing you want, tens of millions of unlabelled images, and no realistic way to hand-label enough data for ordinary supervised learning. AnomalyMatch reframes this as binary classification and applies **FixMatch**, a semi-supervised technique that trains on weakly and strongly augmented views of unlabelled images and keeps confident pseudo-labels, with an **EfficientNet** classifier. Starting from roughly five to ten labelled anomalies, the reported benchmark performance was an average AUROC of 0.96 on miniImageNet and 0.89 on GalaxyMNIST. **Active learning** closes the loop: a human reviews the top-ranked candidates through a GUI, confirms the real ones, corrects the false positives, and the model retrains. In the Hubble run, this is what removed the flood of dense star fields (globular clusters, Andromeda) that the first pass mistook for extended sources.

**The scale.** Training took under four hours on a GPU. Inference over 99.6 million cutouts, drawn from ACS/WFC F814W observations across the whole Hubble Legacy Archive, took two to three days on ESA Datalabs. The authors then personally eyeballed the 5,000 top-ranked candidates. That last step matters: the headline number is not an unsupervised output, it is a human-verified subset of a machine-ranked shortlist.

**What came after.** The tool is open source on GitHub under ESA's organization, and has since been applied to JWST NIRCam data (ASTRODEEP and COSMOS-Web) in a 2026 preprint by Dima, O'Ryan, Kruk, Ruhberg and Gómez reporting 58 gravitational lenses, 37 of them previously uncatalogued. The stated motivation throughout is the upcoming survey era, where Euclid, Rubin and Roman will produce volumes no manual or citizen-science inspection campaign can cover.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
A 35-year-old, heavily studied public archive was searched end to end for the first time, in days, and gave up hundreds of objects nobody had written about. It is a clean demonstration that the bottleneck in archival astronomy is inspection throughput, not data, and that a small semi-supervised model with a human in the loop can move that bottleneck by orders of magnitude.

## Honest caveats
- **These are candidates, not confirmations.** The paper is explicit about its gravitational lenses: the authors "did not attempt to model and confirm them," so these are candidate lenses requiring follow-up observations or lens modelling. The same applies broadly. Nothing here has been spectroscopically confirmed.
- **"Anomalous-looking" is not "scientifically new."** The selection criterion is visual strangeness in a greyscale cutout. A striking morphology can be a projection effect, an overlapping pair, a known object seen unusually, or an artifact.
- **"Undocumented in the literature" is a weak novelty test.** Novelty was assessed by a 3-arcsecond cone search in SIMBAD and ESASky, asking whether any source in that radius was associated with any published work. 811 of 1,339 came back with nothing. That means nobody has written specifically about those coordinates, not that a new class of object was found. It will also miss objects catalogued outside those services, and can misfire on crowded fields.
- **The pipeline surfaces junk too.** About 10% of the 1,339 were classified on inspection as nominal images, small sources, imaging artifacts, or star fields. Note the scoping: that contamination sits *inside* the 1,339 and was not filtered out before it. Table 1 assigns 163 of the 1,339 to the "Nominal Galaxy" class, and the Zenodo release correspondingly describes "1,176 unique objects across 18 different anomaly classifications." Archival anomaly detection reliably finds detector and processing defects alongside astrophysics.
- **Real selection effects.** The search used ACS/WFC F814W data only, so objects distinctive at other wavelengths or from other instruments are underrepresented. Cutouts were JPEG-compressed and greyscale, which the authors note costs low-surface-brightness detail, exactly the kind of feature that defines jellyfish galaxy tails. Large extended sources were "shredded" into as many as five entries in the Hubble Source Catalogue, requiring deduplication. And the Hubble archive is a pointing history, not a survey: it covers where astronomers asked to look, so the sample is not a fair draw from the sky.
- **Numbers differ between the preprint and the paper, and even within the paper.** The published A&A abstract reports 86 new candidate gravitational lenses, consistent with Table 1 (140 lenses found, 54 already referenced). But the "Our contributions" list in the paper's own introduction says 138 candidate lenses, and the current arXiv version (v3, 30 October 2025) still reports 138 in its abstract - so this is a live, unresolved discrepancy between arXiv and A&A, not a superseded early draft. The body of the paper also reports larger per-category totals of which the abstract numbers are the previously-unreferenced subset. Press coverage rounds 1,339 up to "nearly 1,400" and 811 to "more than 800." Use the published paper's numbers.
- **The 43 unclassifiable objects are an open question, not a result.** The authors describe them as having morphologies defying classification and note some may not be galaxies at all, saying they have limited expertise in classifying them. They released them for the community to look at. That is the honest status: interesting, unexplained, unstudied.
