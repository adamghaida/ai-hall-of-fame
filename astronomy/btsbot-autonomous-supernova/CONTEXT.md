# 📚 Context: BTSbot and the autonomous supernova loop

**The people.** **Nabeel Rehemtulla** (Northwestern University, Department of Physics and Astronomy, CIERA, and the NSF-Simons AI Institute for the Sky) is first author of both papers; **Adam A. Miller** (Northwestern) leads the group. The system paper (*ApJ* 972, 7, 2024; arXiv:2401.15167, submitted 26 January 2024) has thirteen authors including Theophile Jegou Du Laz, Michael W. Coughlin, Christoffer Fremling, Daniel A. Perley, Yu-Jing Qin, Jesper Sollerman, Ashish A. Mahabal, Russ R. Laher, Reed Riddle, Ben Rusholme and **Shrinivas R. Kulkarni** (Caltech), who is also senior author of the SN 2024jlf paper (*ApJ* 985, 241, published 29 May 2025; arXiv:2501.18686, v1 30 January 2025, v2 21 April 2025) alongside W. V. Jacobson-Galán, Avinash Singh, Charles D. Kilpatrick and about 25 others. Northwestern announced the SN 2023tyk milestone on 13 October 2023.

**The problem.** ZTF's Bright Transient Survey tries to build a complete, spectroscopically classified sample of every extragalactic transient brighter than 18.5 mag. That completeness is what makes BTS useful for rates and demographics, but it depends on someone looking at every candidate and requesting spectra. Human scanning introduces hours of delay and years of labour (Northwestern cites about 2,200 hours over six years), and the delay is scientifically costly: the first day after a supernova explodes carries information about the progenitor's surroundings that vanishes quickly.

**How BTSbot works.** BTSbot is a multi-modal convolutional neural network. It takes the image cutouts of a ZTF alert together with alert metadata, and outputs a score for "real bright extragalactic transient". It was trained on about 1.4 million historical ZTF images. Integrated into the Fritz marshal and the Kowalski alert database, it scans alerts continuously; above threshold it saves the source and automatically requests a spectrum from SEDM, the robotic spectrograph on the Palomar 60-inch. SEDM's pipeline reduces the spectrum and SNIascore classifies Type Ia supernovae, and the classification is posted publicly. In the system paper, BTSbot recovered all bright transients in the test split, performed on par with scanners, and acted about an hour sooner; in live operation during October 2023 it selected 296 sources, 93% of them real extragalactic transients. SN 2023tyk was the first source to go through the whole chain without a human: ZTF detection on 3 October 2023, BTSbot trigger on 5 October, public Type Ia classification on 7 October.

**BTSbot-nearby.** The 2025 paper repurposes the model for speed rather than completeness. An automated filter in Fritz selects alerts with a BTSbot score of at least 0.5, coincident with a catalogued galaxy closer than 60 Mpc (offset of at most 15 kpc, capped at 2 arcminutes), an absolute magnitude brighter than about minus 11 to reject stellar variables, and a non-detection within the preceding 3.5 days to ensure youth. Matching alerts fire target-of-opportunity requests to SEDM and other facilities at once. SN 2024jlf, a Type IIP supernova at redshift 0.00592 and 18.45 ± 3.7 Mpc, was triggered about 7 minutes after the ZTF detection, roughly 2.5 hours before human scanners saw it, and had its first spectrum 0.7 days after first light. That spectrum showed weak, narrow flash-ionisation lines of Hα, He II and C IV that lasted only 1.3 to 1.8 days, which the paper uses to constrain the progenitor's mass loss. The paper reports that BTSbot has issued more than 1,200 automated spectroscopic follow-up requests since its October 2023 deployment, with about 96% of selections being genuine extragalactic transients.

**Openness.** The code is on GitHub under an MIT licence, installable with pip, with pre-trained models on the Hugging Face Hub and the training set on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.10839690).

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
It is the documented first case of an AI system closing the full loop in observational astronomy, discovery to follow-up observation to classification to public announcement, on a live survey, and then running in production for years with a measurable scientific result (the SN 2024jlf early spectrum) that human latency would have missed.

## Honest caveats
- **"Fully automated" describes the decision chain, not the whole of science.** Humans built the filters, set the thresholds, and still vet the BTS sample afterwards. Classification depends on SEDM obtaining a usable spectrum, which weather and scheduling can prevent.
- **Latency floor.** BTSbot-nearby requires two detections before triggering, which the authors say adds an hour or more; the 7-minute figure is measured from the qualifying ZTF detection, not from the explosion.
- **What the model cannot do.** It cannot on its own reject moving objects (asteroids, satellites); incomplete host-galaxy catalogues (missing distances or redshifts) cause real nearby transients to be missed, which the authors list as a limitation.
- **Scope.** BTSbot targets bright (18.5 mag or brighter) extragalactic transients in ZTF data. Fainter or non-ZTF transients are outside its remit, and the completeness figures apply to the BTS regime.
- **Physics of SN 2024jlf is model-dependent.** The paper notes discrepancies between its mass-loss models and the observations (one model rises too slowly in the optical and overestimates UV flux), and that higher-resolution spectra would have been needed to settle some line-profile questions. The autonomy result does not depend on those conclusions.
- **The "first" claim comes from the team.** The statement that SN 2023tyk was the first fully automatic end-to-end discovery and classification of a transient is made in the system paper and the Northwestern release; we did not find a contradicting prior claim, but have not independently surveyed every project.
