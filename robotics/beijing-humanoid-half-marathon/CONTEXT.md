# 📚 Context: the 2026 Beijing humanoid half-marathon

**The event.** Beijing E-Town (the Beijing Economic-Technological Development Area) added a humanoid-robot division to its half-marathon in April 2025, billed as the first human-robot co-run marathon. Robots run the same 21.0975 km course as the human field but on a separated lane, start at intervals, and are followed by engineers. The 2025 race had 21 robot entries and 6 finishers; the winner, Tiangong Ultra from the Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center, took about 2 hours 40 minutes under remote control, and Al Jazeera's report catalogued robots collapsing at the gun and running into barriers.

**The 2026 rules.** Per the Beijing government's official report: two categories, fully autonomous and remotely controlled; remote-controlled times multiplied by 1.2; 30-second start intervals; judges in golf carts watching for rule violations and battery swaps; RFID chips and pressure mats for timing; more than ten terrain types on the course. Wikipedia adds that up to three battery changes were allowed. More than 100 teams entered (Wikipedia counts over 300 individual robots), from 11 Chinese provinces plus Germany, France, Portugal and Brazil per Smithsonian.

**The winner.** "Lightning" (Shandian) is a running-specialised humanoid from Honor, the Shenzhen smartphone company spun out of Huawei in 2020, entered under the team name Qitian Dasheng ("Monkey King"). Scientific American describes long legs modelled on elite distance runners and a liquid-circulation cooling system adapted from Honor's phones. It finished in 50:26; Honor robots also took second (50:56) and third (53:01). A few months later, at the August 2026 World Humanoid Robot Games, the same robot ran 9.47 s in the 100 m.

**The human record it beat.** Jacob Kiplimo ran 57:20 in Lisbon on March 8, 2026, taking ten seconds off Yomif Kejelcha's 57:30 (Kiplimo's 56:42 in Barcelona the previous year was not ratifiable). World Athletics listed the 57:20 as subject to ratification at the time of the Beijing race. So "faster than the human world record" is true for the winning time, with the differences in conditions listed below.

## Why it's in the Hall of Fame
Because the same race, run one year apart, gives a rare like-for-like measurement of progress in learned humanoid locomotion: 2:40 to 50:26 for the winner, 6 finishers out of 21 to a podium sweep from a field of over 100 teams, no autonomous entries to 38%. The result is a demonstration, not useful work, and the entry says so; but the demonstration was public, timed, and repeated.

## Honest caveats
- **Lightning's autonomy status is not stated on the official page.** The Beijing government report names the winner and the rules but does not say which category Lightning ran in. Wikipedia lists it as autonomous, and Smithsonian reports that a remotely controlled robot crossed first in 48:19 while "an android that figured out the race path itself" was crowned champion, which is consistent with Lightning being autonomous under the 1.2x weighting. We could not find the organiser's own statement.
- **It fell.** Scientific American and Smithsonian both report that Lightning crashed into a barricade and fell in the final stretch and was set upright by handlers. The rules allowed this; a human runner who needed to be picked up would not have been.
- **Battery swaps were allowed** (up to three per Wikipedia; monitored by judges per the official report). The number Lightning used is not reported.
- **Not a comparable race.** Robots ran on a closed, pre-mapped lane, started at intervals rather than in a pack, and were followed by engineering teams with equipment. The human record was set under World Athletics rules.
- **Purpose-built for running.** Lightning is a sprinting and running platform, not a general-purpose humanoid, and its cooling and leg design are specific to this task.
- **Most of the field was still remote-controlled.** Organisers put autonomous entries at 38% (NBC News: nearly 40%). Multiple robots fell or malfunctioned during the race.
- **Expert scepticism is on the record.** Rodney Brooks (Scientific American): "There is nothing useful that you could use in any application because it shows no safety at all." Alan Fern (Oregon State) said there is "no scientific advance in that aspect of the problem," and that how the capability translates into productivity is "much less obvious."
- **Some figures vary by outlet.** Team counts (over 100 teams officially; 112 in some reports; over 300 robots per Wikipedia) and the autonomous share (38% vs. nearly 40%) differ slightly between sources. We use the official figure where one exists.
